## 2.1 Glacial lakes and outburst floods

### 2.1.1 Global assessment

Floods from glaciers, including glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), have been recorded in most mountain ranges that hold glaciers [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC counts them among the glacier hazards with the longest reach [@hock2019srocc]. Earlier floods of this type reached distances of tens to hundreds of kilometres below their source [@hock2019srocc]. Lakes can be held back by moraines or by glacier ice, and both types have produced floods [@hock2019srocc].

In most regions, glacial lakes have become more numerous and larger over recent decades (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC finds only *limited evidence* of a change in how often GLOFs occur [@hock2019srocc]. Recent trends in the number of glacier floods remain unclear, and records of past events may be far from complete [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC cites a Himalayan study that detected no rise in GLOF numbers from the late 1980s onwards [@hock2019srocc]. Studies have shown that degrading permafrost and melting ice buried within dams weaken those dams [@hock2019srocc].

Over the coming decades, SROCC expects glacial lakes to keep growing in number and area across most regions (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. New lakes will form closer to steep and possibly unstable rock walls, where landslides into the lake can more easily set off an outburst [@hock2019srocc].

### 2.1.2 Exposure

More people and more infrastructure in high mountain regions have been exposed to cryosphere hazards over recent decades, a trend expected to continue (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Tourism and hydropower are two drivers [@hock2019srocc]. As much as two-thirds of hydropower projects in the Himalaya, built or planned, are sited where potential glacier floods could reach them [@hock2019srocc].

## 2.2 Unstable slopes and hazard cascades

### 2.2.1 Slopes

Mountain slopes have become less stable as glaciers retreat and permafrost thaws (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Over the last fifty years, rock fall from steep slopes has become more frequent where permafrost is degrading (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Field evidence and theory agree that thawing permafrost makes rock falls and larger rock avalanches more likely [@hock2019srocc]. Glacier retreat has also destabilised nearby rock and debris slopes in most high mountains, over periods from years to millennia (*high confidence*), although robust statistics on current trends are lacking [@hock2019srocc].

### 2.2.2 Cascades

The largest mountain disasters involving ice, snow and permafrost, in terms of reach, damage and deaths, have occurred through chains of processes [@hock2019srocc]. Outbursts of glacial lakes have been set off by waves from avalanches and landslides falling into them [@hock2019srocc]. Landslides and rock avalanches that take up snow, ice, loose sediment or water can travel further and grow in volume [@hock2019srocc]. There is *limited evidence* of observed event chains on which to base projections of future trends [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC projects that landslides, floods and cascading events will also occur in places with no record of earlier events (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc].

The researchers reconstructing the Blatten event describe it as a hazard cascade [@egu26-19777]. The Thame flood also followed a chain of linked failures [@icimod2025thame]. Both began with a rock failure [@egu26-19777; @icimod2025thame].

## 2.3 Case study: Blatten, Switzerland, 28 May 2025

### 2.3.1 Sequence of events

Blatten is a village in the Lötschental, in Valais, Switzerland [@egu26-19777; @egu26-6599]. Above it, the rock slope of the Kleines Nesthorn began to deform faster about two weeks before the main event [@egu26-6599]. In late May 2025, a series of large rock failures fell from the Kleines Nesthorn onto the Birch Glacier (Birchgletscher) [@egu26-19777]. They placed about 4 million m³ of rock on the glacier [@egu26-19777].

The glacier then accelerated for several days [@egu26-19777]. On 28 May 2025 it collapsed completely [@egu26-19777]. The collapse produced a rock and ice avalanche of more than 9 million m³, with about three parts rock to one part ice [@egu26-19777]. A second abstract describes a debris and ice avalanche of approximately 10 million m³ that moved in two phases [@egu26-6599]. Debris up to 32 m thick was deposited on the village [@egu26-6599].

The avalanche blocked the river Lonza [@egu26-19777]. The lake that formed behind the deposit damaged further parts of the village [@egu26-19777]. Bellwald lists the formation of this lake up-valley, and of a new river bed, among the stages of the event [@egu26-6599].

### 2.3.2 Consequences

The village of Blatten was almost entirely destroyed [@egu26-19777]. It had been fully evacuated before the collapse [@egu26-19777]. The two abstracts describe casualties in different terms [@egu26-19777; @egu26-6599]. One reports that the collapse claimed one life [@egu26-19777]. The other credits the monitoring and mitigation measures of the authorities with allowing residents to leave in time, and refers to loss of life having been avoided [@egu26-6599].

### 2.3.3 Causes under study

Bellwald considers that the rock faces of the Kleines Nesthorn were "very likely" affected by permafrost, on the basis of their exposure and altitude [@egu26-6599]. He names two factors as "most likely" the main causes: "the unfavourable geology in combination with melting permafrost", and increased hydrostatic pressures [@egu26-6599]. These are the author's own assessments, not IPCC calibrated terms. He also suggests that heavy precipitation on 28 May 2025 probably saturated the debris on the glacier, which may partly explain how far the avalanche travelled [@egu26-6599]. He notes that the roles of geology and climate in preparing and triggering the event are still debated [@egu26-6599].

Jacquemart and colleagues use weather station records, satellite data and climate models to assess the role of human-caused climate change in the state of the glacier, snow cover, permafrost and the chain of processes [@egu26-19777]. Their abstract does not state a conclusion on this question [@egu26-19777].

This report therefore does not attribute the Blatten event to climate change.

## 2.4 Case study: Thame, Nepal, 16 August 2024

### 2.4.1 Sequence of events

Thame is a village in the Everest region of Nepal [@icimod2025press]. On 16 August 2024, two glacial lakes about 10 km upstream of the village failed one after the other: Upper Ngole Cho and Lower Ngole Cho [@icimod2025thame].

The event began with a rock avalanche into Upper Ngole Cho [@icimod2025thame]. ICIMOD gives the altitude of this first lake as 4,900 metres [@icimod2025press]. The avalanche created a displacement wave that cut through the moraine lying on the lake's bedrock dam, a layer 4.5 m thick [@icimod2025thame]. The first lake released 156,000 m³ of water [@icimod2025press]. The outflow dropped 120 metres, which increased its power to erode [@icimod2025press].

The flow then reached Lower Ngole Cho and caused it to fail [@icimod2025thame]. The breach removed a section of the lake's end moraine up to 22 m in height and 51 m in width [@icimod2025thame]. The second lake released a further 303,000 m³ [@icimod2025press]. The total volume released is estimated at 459,000 m³ [@icimod2025press].

The flood became a hyper-concentrated flow, carrying sediment and large boulders [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. It transported debris more than 80 km along the Thame and Dudh Koshi rivers [@icimod2025thame]. Near Thame, the valley floor is about half a kilometre wide, and the flood covered it with debris [@icimod2025press].

### 2.4.2 Consequences

The flood displaced 135 people [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. It destroyed 25 homes [@icimod2025press]. It also destroyed or damaged farmland, hotels, a school, a health post, a bridge and hydropower facilities [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. The study reports widespread panic among communities further downstream [@icimod2025thame]. After the flood, sediment deposits, erosion, subsidence and tension cracks made the ground under the village less stable [@icimod2025thame].

The flood caused no deaths, which ICIMOD links to two factors: it happened in daylight, and it arrived in phases because floodwater ponded temporarily [@icimod2025press].

### 2.4.3 Causes and remaining risk

A co-author of the study, Sudan Bikash Maharjan, describes Thame as a climate-driven event whose damage was made worse by the geology and landforms of the valley [@icimod2025press]. He adds that it shows the need to understand risks from relatively small lakes as well as large ones [@icimod2025press]. The study's own assessment of causes, as summarised in its record, is the sequence of rock avalanche, displacement wave and successive dam breaches set out above [@icimod2025thame].

The study assesses the lakes that remain in the catchment [@icimod2025thame]:

- Upper Ngole Cho remains highly vulnerable to future avalanches and overflow.
- Rindhi Cho is high risk because it is dammed by unstable ice.
- Homey Cho is moderate risk and needs detailed investigation.
