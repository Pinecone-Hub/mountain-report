## 3.1 Monitoring glaciers and mountain conditions

Field measurements of glacier mass change give annual values but cover roughly 500 glaciers, under 0.2% of the world's total [@dussaillant2025essd]. Satellite and airborne surveys cover about 96% of glaciers, but over periods of several years [@dussaillant2025essd]. Combining the two allows annual estimates for every glacier [@dussaillant2025essd]. The resulting dataset is open access, on condition that it is cited correctly [@wgms2026amce].

Not every method can be used everywhere. GlaMBIE did not use satellite gravimetry for three regions of High Mountain Asia (Central Asia, South Asia West and South Asia East), nor for regions where glaciers are small [@glambie2025]. Its combined estimate drew on 233 regional estimates from 35 research teams [@glambie2025].

SROCC identifies gaps in high mountain observation. Observation records at high elevations are sparse and short, which limits the detection and attribution of changes in their atmospheric drivers [@hock2019srocc]. Knowledge gaps remain on current glacier ice volumes, among other variables [@hock2019srocc]. Satellite data help to overcome the remoteness of high mountains, but cloud cover and rugged terrain still limit them [@hock2019srocc]. Citizen science, and Indigenous and local knowledge, can complement observations from instruments and models [@hock2019srocc]. For mountain communities, SROCC notes a lack of the basic weather and climate information needed both for short-term warning of disasters and for long-term adaptation planning [@hock2019srocc].

## 3.2 Monitoring hazards and warning

### 3.2.1 What SROCC finds on early warning

SROCC finds that early warning systems depend on strong local engagement and on building local capacity [@hock2019srocc]. Communities need to know how to prepare and respond, and projects need to last [@hock2019srocc]. In Pakistan and Chile, glacier flood warnings, evacuation and relief after disasters have largely been led by communities [@hock2019srocc].

In the Cordillera Blanca of Peru, warning systems have been installed, or are being installed, at the glacial lakes Laguna 513 and Palcacocha [@hock2019srocc]. Laguna 513 was lowered by 20 m in the early 1990s to prevent outbursts [@hock2019srocc]. It still caused a destructive flood in 2010, but that flood was much smaller than it would have been without the earlier works [@hock2019srocc]. A warning system installed after 2010 was destroyed by some local residents in 2017, because of political, social and cultural conflicts [@hock2019srocc]. Experience in Peru has shown the value of education and communication at community level for advanced warning systems [@hock2019srocc].

### 3.2.2 Blatten

At Blatten, the rock slope of the Kleines Nesthorn began to deform faster about two weeks before the collapse [@egu26-6599]. The Birch Glacier then accelerated for several days before it failed on 28 May 2025 [@egu26-19777]. The village had been fully evacuated by the time of the collapse [@egu26-19777]. Bellwald attributes the timely evacuation of residents to the monitoring and mitigation work of the authorities [@egu26-6599].

The abstracts do not describe the monitoring system, the warning thresholds or the evacuation orders [@egu26-19777; @egu26-6599].

### 3.2.3 Thame

Neither the ICIMOD press release nor the published summary of the study (its library record) says whether a warning system was in place at Thame, or whether a warning was issued before the flood [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. The flood caused no deaths, which ICIMOD attributes to the flood occurring in daylight and to its arrival in phases, due to the temporary ponding of floodwater [@icimod2025press]. The press release does not link the absence of deaths to a warning [@icimod2025press].

## 3.3 Hazard mapping, land-use planning and risk reduction

### 3.3.1 Options assessed by SROCC

SROCC lists the adaptation strategies that have been applied to floods and landslides in high mountains [@hock2019srocc]:

- engineering works that reduce the hazard, such as lowering the level of glacial lakes, channel engineering and slope stabilisation;
- nature-based solutions, such as planting vegetation on unstable slopes or channels;
- hazard and risk mapping, which provides the basis for land zoning and early warning systems that reduce exposure;
- community programmes for disaster response, local capacity and lower vulnerability.

In Nepal, a channel dug at Tsho Rolpa lowered the lake at a cost of 3 million USD in 2000, and similar works were carried out at Imja Tsho in 2016 [@hock2019srocc]. In the countries with glacier-covered mountain ranges where adaptation action has been identified, it has mostly been reactive, rather than based on formal plans made in advance [@hock2019srocc]. The scientific literature on lessons learned from adaptation remains scarce [@hock2019srocc].

Effective governance is a key enabler for reducing disaster risk, taking account of exposure factors such as planning, zoning and urbanisation pressures, and of vulnerability factors such as poverty (*medium confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Looking at hazard, exposure and vulnerability together, from a combined social and ecological perspective, opens the way to adaptation strategies that address all three (*medium confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. With hazards, exposure and vulnerability growing in the coming century, significant risk reduction and adaptation will be needed to avoid greater impacts (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc].

### 3.3.2 Blatten

The village of Blatten has existed since at least 1433 [@egu26-6599]. Its records name snow avalanches and floods as the most common hazards, with recurrence intervals of 2 and 16 years for the Lötschental [@egu26-6599]. They contain no rock avalanches [@egu26-6599].

The geohazard map for Blatten was updated in November 2025 [@egu26-6599]. According to Bellwald, the new map supports plans to rebuild a safe village, referred to as "Blatten 2.0", with building land in areas of moderate hazard [@egu26-6599].

### 3.3.3 Thame

The study recommends that very high-resolution survey data be used for land-use planning and for hydropower infrastructure [@icimod2025thame]. It also recommends a long-term approach that combines structural and non-structural measures [@icimod2025thame]. ICIMOD calls for a flood risk management plan for the valley [@icimod2025press]. For the lakes that remain, the study finds Upper Ngole Cho still highly vulnerable to avalanches and overflow, rates Rindhi Cho as high risk and rates Homey Cho as moderate risk in need of detailed investigation (see section 2.4.3) [@icimod2025thame].

## 3.4 Response and recovery

### 3.4.1 Immediate measures at Thame

The flood left the ground at Thame unstable, with sediment deposits, erosion, subsidence and tension cracks [@icimod2025thame]. The study sets out immediate measures for the site [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]:

- fill the cracks that opened in the ground, and keep flood water, rain and meltwater from running or seeping into them;
- support and stabilise riverbanks where the ground is subsiding;
- channel the river flow;
- build bank protection to reduce damage from future floods;
- control erosion.

ICIMOD also calls for action on risk before events occur rather than after, and for cryosphere and disaster science to be taken up in policy [@icimod2025press].

### 3.4.2 Rebuilding Blatten

Bellwald describes a plan under which residents would return to a rebuilt Blatten by 2029 [@egu26-6599]. He identifies insurance, donations and special regulations for Blatten as factors that support their willingness to return [@egu26-6599]. He also notes that the plan depends on its timetable being kept [@egu26-6599].

### 3.4.3 Reconstruction as a chance to reduce risk

SROCC notes that the period of recovery and reconstruction can be used to build resilience [@hock2019srocc]. It gives the example of the 2013 Kedarnath glacier flood in India, after which guest houses and even schools were rebuilt in the same exposed places [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC also reports suggestions that responses should include forward planning and the anticipation of emerging risks [@hock2019srocc]. For mountain regions, combining knowledge and practice from the natural and social sciences and the humanities is most efficient in addressing complex hazards related to glaciers, snow and permafrost (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc].

## 3.5 What the cases show about warning and response

The sources describe the lead-up to the two events in very different detail. For Blatten, they record faster deformation of the rock slope about two weeks before the collapse, and the evacuation of the village before the glacier failed [@egu26-6599; @egu26-19777]. Bellwald links the timely evacuation to monitoring and mitigation by the authorities [@egu26-6599]. For Thame, the press release and the published summary of the study say nothing about the lead-up or about any warning [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. The absence of deaths is linked to the timing and phased nature of the flood [@icimod2025press].

Both sources also draw lessons about the limits of what was known before the event. At Blatten, rock avalanches were absent from the historical records [@egu26-6599]. At Thame, a co-author of the study points to the need to understand the risk from relatively small lakes [@icimod2025press].

Both cases led to a reassessment of hazard after the event. At Blatten this took the form of an updated geohazard map [@egu26-6599]. At Thame it took the form of a risk assessment of the remaining lakes and a set of recommendations on monitoring, early warning and planning [@icimod2025thame].

These observations come from two cases only. They do not support general conclusions about early warning in high mountain regions. Sections 3.2.1 and 3.3.1 summarise the general assessment in SROCC.
