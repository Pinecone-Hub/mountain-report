## 2.1 Glacial lakes and outburst floods

In the Thame glacial lake outburst flood described in section 2.4, the flood followed the failure of the moraine dams of two glacial lakes [@icimod2025thame].

### 2.1.1 Global assessment

Floods from glaciers, including glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), have been recorded in most mountain ranges that hold glaciers [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC counts them among the glacier hazards with the longest reach: past floods have affected areas tens to hundreds of kilometres downstream [@hock2019srocc]. Lakes can be held back by moraines or by glacier ice, and both types have produced floods [@hock2019srocc].

The number and area of glacial lakes have increased in most regions in recent decades (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. There is, however, only *limited evidence* that the frequency of GLOFs has changed [@hock2019srocc]. Trends in the number of glacier floods in recent decades are not well known, and event inventories may miss many floods [@hock2019srocc]. For the Himalaya, SROCC reports a study that found no increase in the number of GLOFs since the late 1980s [@hock2019srocc]. Thawing permafrost and the melting of ice buried in lake dams have made some dams less stable [@hock2019srocc].

SROCC projects that the number and area of glacial lakes will continue to increase in most regions in the coming decades (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. New lakes will form closer to steep and possibly unstable rock walls, where landslides into the lake can more easily set off an outburst [@hock2019srocc].

### 2.1.2 Exposure and losses

The exposure of people and infrastructure to cryosphere hazards in high mountain regions has increased over recent decades, and this trend is expected to continue (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Tourism and hydropower are two drivers [@hock2019srocc]. In the Himalaya, up to two-thirds of current and planned hydropower projects lie in the path of potential glacier floods [@hock2019srocc].

Over the past two centuries, GLOFs have directly caused at least 400 deaths in Europe, 5,745 in South America and 6,300 in Asia [@hock2019srocc]. A few large events account for much of these totals [@hock2019srocc]. Nepal and Bhutan have had the largest economic impacts from glacier floods at national level [@hock2019srocc].

### 2.1.3 The Hindu Kush Himalaya

The Hindu Kush Himalaya region has more than 25,000 glacial lakes, spread across the Amu Darya, Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra and Irrawaddy basins [@icimod2025press]. Nepal has recorded more than 90 GLOFs since the early 1920s [@icimod2025press]. The Everest region alone has had five significant GLOFs in less than 50 years, among them the Dig Tsho flood of 1985 in a valley next to Thame [@icimod2025press]. SROCC reports that the Dig Tsho flood damaged a hydropower plant and other property, with estimated losses of 500 million USD [@hock2019srocc].

## 2.2 Unstable slopes, glacier collapse and hazard cascades

### 2.2.1 Slopes and glaciers

Glacier retreat and permafrost thaw have reduced the stability of mountain slopes (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Rock fall from steep slopes has become more frequent in zones of degrading permafrost over the past half-century (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. Field evidence and theory agree that thawing permafrost makes rock falls and larger rock avalanches more likely [@hock2019srocc]. Glacier retreat has also destabilised nearby rock and debris slopes in most high mountains, over periods from years to millennia (*high confidence*), although robust statistics on current trends are lacking [@hock2019srocc].

Ice breaking off steep glacier fronts is a natural process, and too few observations exist to detect trends [@hock2019srocc]. Where steep glaciers are frozen to their beds, warmer basal ice has made, and will make, large failures easier (*medium evidence, high agreement*) [@hock2019srocc].

SROCC describes a rare type of instability in which large sections of low-angle valley glaciers collapse completely [@hock2019srocc]. These collapses produce combined avalanches of ice, rock and debris with volumes of the order of 10 to 100 million m³ and speeds of up to 200 to 300 km/h [@hock2019srocc]. The largest reported cases were at Kolka Glacier in the Caucasus in 2002, with about 130 deaths, and two glaciers in the Aru Range in Tibet in 2016, with 9 deaths [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC found no evidence of a direct role of climate change in the 2002 event [@hock2019srocc]. Changes linked at least partly to climate change were involved in the 2016 collapses [@hock2019srocc]. Apart from the 2016 cases, it is not known whether such collapses can be attributed to climate change [@hock2019srocc].

### 2.2.2 Cascades

The largest mountain disasters involving ice, snow and permafrost, in terms of reach, damage and deaths, have occurred through chains of processes [@hock2019srocc]. Outbursts of glacial lakes have been set off by waves from avalanches and landslides falling into them [@hock2019srocc]. Landslides and rock avalanches that take up snow, ice, loose sediment or water can travel further and grow in volume [@hock2019srocc]. There is *limited evidence* of observed event chains on which to base projections of future trends [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC projects that landslides, floods and cascading events will also occur in places with no record of earlier events (*high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc].

The researchers reconstructing the Blatten event describe it as a hazard cascade [@egu26-19777]. The Thame flood also followed a chain of linked failures [@icimod2025thame]. Both began with a rock failure [@egu26-19777; @icimod2025thame].

## 2.3 Case study: Blatten, Switzerland, 28 May 2025

### 2.3.1 Sequence of events

Blatten is a village in the Lötschental, in Valais, Switzerland [@egu26-19777; @egu26-6599]. Above it, the rock slope of the Kleines Nesthorn began to deform faster about two weeks before the main event [@egu26-6599]. In late May 2025, a series of large rock failures fell from the Kleines Nesthorn onto the Birch Glacier (Birchgletscher) [@egu26-19777]. They placed about 4 million m³ of rock on the glacier [@egu26-19777].

The glacier then accelerated for several days [@egu26-19777]. On 28 May 2025 it collapsed completely [@egu26-19777]. The collapse produced a rock and ice avalanche of more than 9 million m³, with about three parts rock to one part ice [@egu26-19777]. A second abstract describes a debris and ice avalanche of approximately 10 million m³ that moved in two phases [@egu26-6599]. Debris up to 32 m thick was deposited on the village [@egu26-6599].

The avalanche blocked the river Lonza [@egu26-19777]. A lake formed upstream of the deposit and flooded further parts of the village [@egu26-19777; @egu26-6599]. The impounded water later flowed out and cut a new river bed [@egu26-6599].

### 2.3.2 Consequences

The village of Blatten was almost entirely destroyed [@egu26-19777]. It had been fully evacuated before the collapse [@egu26-19777]. One abstract reports that the collapse claimed one life [@egu26-19777]. The other credits the monitoring and mitigation measures of the authorities with allowing residents to leave in time, and refers to loss of life having been avoided [@egu26-6599]. Neither abstract states the number of people evacuated or the time of the evacuation.

### 2.3.3 Causes under study

Bellwald considers that the rock faces of the Kleines Nesthorn were "very likely" affected by permafrost, on the basis of their exposure and altitude [@egu26-6599]. In his view, melting permafrost and higher hydrostatic pressure in the rock were "most likely" the main causes of the failure [@egu26-6599]. These are the author's own assessments, not IPCC calibrated terms. He also suggests that heavy precipitation on 28 May 2025 probably saturated the debris on the glacier, which may partly explain how far the avalanche travelled [@egu26-6599]. He notes that the roles of geology and climate in preparing and triggering the event are still debated [@egu26-6599].

Jacquemart and colleagues use weather station records, satellite data and climate models to assess the role of human-caused climate change in the state of the glacier, snow cover, permafrost and the chain of processes [@egu26-19777]. Their abstract does not state a conclusion on this question [@egu26-19777]. They also reconstruct the history of the glacier from 1946 to 2023, which included substantial mass loss and a recent surge-type acceleration [@egu26-19777].

This report therefore does not attribute the Blatten event to climate change.

## 2.4 Case study: Thame, Nepal, 16 August 2024

### 2.4.1 Sequence of events

Thame is a village in the Everest region of Nepal [@icimod2025press]. On 16 August 2024, two glacial lakes about 10 km upstream of the village failed one after the other: Upper Ngole Cho and Lower Ngole Cho [@icimod2025thame].

The event began with a rock avalanche into Upper Ngole Cho [@icimod2025thame]. ICIMOD gives the altitude of this first lake as 4,900 metres [@icimod2025press]. The avalanche created a displacement wave that eroded the lake's moraine dam [@icimod2025thame]. The first lake released 156,000 m³ of water [@icimod2025press]. The outflow dropped 120 metres, which increased its power to erode [@icimod2025press].

The flow then reached Lower Ngole Cho and caused it to fail [@icimod2025thame]. The breach cut up to 22 m deep and 51 m wide into the end moraine [@icimod2025thame]. The second lake released a further 303,000 m³ [@icimod2025press]. The total volume released is estimated at 459,000 m³ [@icimod2025press].

The flood became a hyper-concentrated flow, carrying sediment and large boulders [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. It transported debris more than 80 km along the Thame and Dudh Koshi rivers [@icimod2025thame]. Below the Thyanbo outwash plain the river channel narrows naturally [@icimod2025press]. This narrowing, together with the draining of floodwater that had ponded temporarily, made the flow more erosive [@icimod2025press]. Near Thame, the valley floor is about half a kilometre wide, and the flood covered it with debris [@icimod2025press].

### 2.4.2 Consequences

The flood displaced 135 people [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. It destroyed 25 homes [@icimod2025press]. It also destroyed or damaged farmland, hotels, a school, a health post, a bridge and hydropower facilities [@icimod2025thame; @icimod2025press]. After the flood, sediment deposits, erosion, subsidence and tension cracks made the ground under the village less stable [@icimod2025thame].

The flood caused no deaths, which ICIMOD links to two factors: it happened in daylight, and it arrived in phases because floodwater ponded temporarily [@icimod2025press].

### 2.4.3 Causes and remaining risk

A co-author of the study, Sudan Bikash Maharjan, describes Thame as a climate-driven event whose damage was made worse by the geology and landforms of the valley [@icimod2025press]. He adds that it shows the need to understand risks from relatively small lakes as well as large ones [@icimod2025press]. The study's own assessment of causes, as summarised in its record, is the sequence of rock avalanche, displacement wave and moraine failure set out above [@icimod2025thame].

The study assesses the lakes that remain in the catchment [@icimod2025thame]:

- Upper Ngole Cho remains highly exposed to future avalanches and overflow.
- Rindhi Cho is high risk because it is dammed by unstable ice.
- Homey Cho is moderate risk and needs detailed investigation.

## 2.5 What the two cases have in common

Both events started with a rock failure and grew through a sequence of linked processes [@egu26-19777; @icimod2025thame]. At Blatten, rock loading led to the collapse of a glacier and a river-blocking avalanche [@egu26-19777]. At Thame, a rock avalanche led to the failure of two lakes in turn [@icimod2025thame]. In both cases the most damaging effects occurred well below the starting point: the burial and flooding of Blatten, and debris transport over more than 80 km below Thame [@egu26-6599; @icimod2025thame]. Chapter 3 considers what the two cases show about monitoring, warning and response.
