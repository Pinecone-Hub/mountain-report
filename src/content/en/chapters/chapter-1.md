## 1.1 How glacier mass change is measured

Four main kinds of observation are used to estimate glacier mass change: field measurements on individual glaciers (the glaciological method), repeated mapping of surface elevation (digital elevation model, or DEM, differencing), satellite altimetry and satellite gravimetry [@glambie2025]. Some estimates combine more than one of these methods [@glambie2025].

Field measurements give annual values, but in situ glaciological series exist for only roughly 500 glaciers, which is under 0.2% of the world's glaciers [@dussaillant2025essd]. Geodetic surveys from satellites and aircraft cover about 207,000 glaciers, or 96% of the total, and their elevation-change estimates are decadal [@dussaillant2025essd].

The World Glacier Monitoring Service (WGMS) produces annual mass-change estimates for every glacier by joining these two types of data [@wgms2026amce; @dussaillant2025essd]. It does so with geostatistical modelling [@dussaillant2025essd]. Annual anomalies measured in the field are interpolated in space and then fitted to the long-term change measured by geodesy for each glacier, which spreads the multi-year geodetic signal over individual years [@dussaillant2025essd]. The geodetic data cover different periods within 2000 to 2019 [@dussaillant2025essd]. Results are produced for each glacier and then combined on a 0.5° grid, by region and for the globe [@wgms2026amce; @dussaillant2025essd]. The current version of the dataset gives global estimates for the hydrological years 1976 to 2025 [@wgms2026amce]. Regional series start earlier, between 1915 and 1976, depending on how much data each region has [@wgms2026amce].

A second approach compares and combines many independent estimates [@glambie2025]. The Glacier Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (GlaMBIE) gathered 233 regional estimates from about 450 data contributors in 35 research teams [@glambie2025]. Of these, 195 were used in the combined results [@glambie2025]. GlaMBIE reports uncertainties as 95% confidence intervals [@glambie2025]. Gravimetry was not used for regions with small glaciers, nor for some regions of High Mountain Asia [@glambie2025].

## 1.2 Global glacier mass change since the 1970s

The paper describing the WGMS method reports that glaciers lost 9,179 ± 621 Gt of water between 1976 and 2024, an average of 187 ± 20 Gt yr⁻¹ [@dussaillant2025essd]. About 41% of this total loss took place in the last ten years of that record [@dussaillant2025essd]. About 6% of it took place in 2023 alone, which the authors identify as a record year for glacier mass loss [@dussaillant2025essd].

The current version of the series runs to the hydrological year 2025 [@wgms2026amce; @zemp2026]. On this basis, glaciers have lost 9,583 ± 1,211 Gt since 1975 [@zemp2026].[^period]

## 1.3 Glacier loss from 2000 to 2023

GlaMBIE estimates that glaciers lost on average 273 ± 16 Gt yr⁻¹ from 2000 to 2023 [@glambie2025]. The cumulative loss over the period was 6,542 ± 387 Gt [@glambie2025]. This is about 5% of the global glacier mass in 2000 [@glambie2025].

Mass loss averaged 231 ± 23 Gt yr⁻¹ in 2000 to 2011 and 314 ± 23 Gt yr⁻¹ in 2012 to 2023, an increase of 36 ± 10% [@glambie2025]. The largest annual loss in the record was in 2023, at 548 ± 120 Gt [@glambie2025].

### 1.3.1 Regional differences

Relative losses since 2000 range from 2% to 39% between regions [@glambie2025]. Central Europe lost 39% of its glacier ice, the largest relative loss of any region [@glambie2025]. Its glaciers lost 2.0 ± 0.1 Gt yr⁻¹, or 1.06 ± 0.04 m w.e. yr⁻¹ (metres water equivalent) averaged over the glacier area [@glambie2025]. In High Mountain Asia, mass loss in South Asia West increased in the second half of the record [@glambie2025]. Over 2000 to 2023, the largest regional shares of global glacier loss came from Alaska (22%), the Canadian Arctic (20%), the glaciers around Greenland (13%) and the Southern Andes (10%) [@glambie2025].

### 1.3.2 The IPCC assessment of high mountain glaciers

The IPCC Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere in a Changing Climate (SROCC) assessed glaciers in eleven high mountain regions [@hock2019srocc]. These cover all glacier regions except Antarctica, Greenland, the Canadian and Russian Arctic, and Svalbard [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC found that glaciers in high mountain areas have declined in recent decades due to climate change (*very high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. In its judgement, warming of the atmosphere is *very likely* the main cause of glacier recession worldwide [@hock2019srocc].

For 2006 to 2015, SROCC assessed the mass budget of glaciers in these mountain regions as *very likely* −490 ± 100 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (−123 ± 24 Gt yr⁻¹) [@hock2019srocc]. Regional averages were *likely* most negative, below −850 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹, in the southern Andes, the Caucasus and the European Alps and Pyrenees [@hock2019srocc]. High Mountain Asia had the least negative average, at −150 ± 110 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹, with strong variation within the region [@hock2019srocc].

## 1.4 Recent years

In the hydrological year 2025, glaciers outside the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets lost 408 ± 132 Gt of mass [@zemp2026]. Six of the years with the highest mass loss on record fall within the past seven years [@zemp2026].

The pattern varied between regions in 2025 [@zemp2026]. Loss per unit area was greatest in Western Canada and USA, Iceland and Central Europe [@zemp2026]. Compared with the 1991 to 2020 climate period, the largest departures from normal were in Western Canada and USA, South Asia West and Svalbard [@zemp2026]. The regions that added most to the global total were High Mountain Asia, Alaska and the Russian Arctic [@zemp2026].

The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) also reports on a set of reference glaciers with long-term measurements [@wmo2026sogc]. For these glaciers, the mass balance of the hydrological year 2024/2025 (September 2024 to August 2025) was among the five most negative in the record for 1950 to 2025 [@wmo2026sogc]. It was less negative than the record loss of 2022/2023 [@wmo2026sogc]. In the WMO record, which begins in 1950, eight of the ten years with the most negative annual balance fall in the period from 2016 onwards [@wmo2026sogc]. WMO identifies Iceland and the Pacific coast of North America as areas of exceptionally negative mass balance in 2025 [@wmo2026sogc].

## 1.5 Contribution to sea-level rise

The paper describing the WGMS method reports that glacier mass loss raised global mean sea level by 25.3 ± 1.7 mm between 1976 and 2024, or 0.5 ± 0.2 mm yr⁻¹ on average [@dussaillant2025essd]. About 10 mm of this rise came in the last ten years of that record, and about 1.5 mm in 2023 [@dussaillant2025essd].

GlaMBIE gives a contribution of 18 ± 1 mm from 2000 to 2023, an average of 0.75 ± 0.04 mm yr⁻¹ [@glambie2025]. The 2023 loss alone corresponds to 1.51 ± 0.33 mm [@glambie2025]. For 2002 to 2021, GlaMBIE puts the glacier contribution at 0.72 ± 0.04 mm yr⁻¹ and that of the Greenland Ice Sheet at 0.62 ± 0.06 mm yr⁻¹ [@glambie2025]. It expresses these as shares of 20% and 17% of observed sea-level rise, using an observed rate of 3.64 ± 0.26 mm yr⁻¹ for 2003 to 2016 [@glambie2025]. Over 2002 to 2021, glaciers lost about 18% more mass than the Greenland Ice Sheet and over twice as much as the Antarctic Ice Sheet [@glambie2025].

The loss in the hydrological year 2025 corresponds to 1.1 ± 0.4 mm of sea-level rise [@zemp2026]. The cumulative loss since 1975 corresponds to 26.4 ± 3.3 mm [@zemp2026].

For the eleven high mountain regions alone, SROCC gives a sea-level contribution of 0.34 ± 0.07 mm yr⁻¹ for 2006 to 2015 [@hock2019srocc].
