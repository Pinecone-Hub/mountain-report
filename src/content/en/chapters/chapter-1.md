## 1.1 How glacier mass change is measured

Glacier mass change is estimated from several kinds of observation. The main ones are field measurements on individual glaciers (the glaciological method), repeated mapping of surface elevation (digital elevation model, or DEM, differencing), satellite altimetry and satellite gravimetry [@glambie2025]. Some estimates combine more than one of these methods [@glambie2025].

Each method has limits. Field measurements give annual values, but in situ glaciological series exist for only roughly 500 glaciers, which is under 0.2% of the world's glaciers [@dussaillant2025essd]. Geodetic surveys from satellites and aircraft cover about 207,000 glaciers, or 96% of the total, but mostly over periods of several years [@dussaillant2025essd].

The World Glacier Monitoring Service (WGMS) produces annual mass-change estimates for every glacier by joining these two types of data [@wgms2026amce; @dussaillant2025essd]. The method uses geostatistical modelling. Annual anomalies measured in the field are interpolated in space and then fitted to the long-term change measured by geodesy for each glacier, which spreads the multi-year geodetic signal over individual years [@dussaillant2025essd]. The geodetic data cover different periods within 2000 to 2019 [@dussaillant2025essd]. Results are produced for each glacier and then combined on a 0.5° grid, by region and for the globe [@wgms2026amce; @dussaillant2025essd]. The current version of the dataset gives global estimates for the hydrological years 1976 to 2025 [@wgms2026amce]. Regional series start earlier, between 1915 and 1976, depending on how much data each region has [@wgms2026amce].

The method is described in a paper that covers the series to 2024 [@dussaillant2025essd]. Its authors tested the method by leaving out single glaciers, and groups of glaciers, and checking how well the model reproduced them [@dussaillant2025essd]. They also compared their results with earlier global assessments [@dussaillant2025essd]. Uncertainties in the paper are given at one standard deviation (a 68% confidence interval) unless otherwise stated [@dussaillant2025essd].

A second approach compares and combines many independent estimates. The Glacier Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (GlaMBIE) gathered 233 regional estimates from about 450 data contributors in 35 research teams [@glambie2025]. Of these, 195 were used in the combined results [@glambie2025]. GlaMBIE reports uncertainties as 95% confidence intervals [@glambie2025]. Gravimetry was not used for regions with small glaciers, nor for some regions of High Mountain Asia [@glambie2025].

The two approaches use different periods and different uncertainty conventions. Their figures are therefore given separately in this chapter.

## 1.2 Global glacier mass change since the 1970s

The paper describing the WGMS method reports that glaciers lost 9,179 ± 621 Gt of water between 1976 and 2024, an average of 187 ± 20 Gt per year [@dussaillant2025essd]. About 41% of this total loss took place in the last ten years of that record [@dussaillant2025essd]. About 6% of it took place in 2023 alone, which the authors identify as a record year for glacier mass loss [@dussaillant2025essd].

The current version of the series runs to the hydrological year 2025 [@wgms2026amce; @zemp2026]. On this basis, glaciers have lost 9,583 ± 1,211 Gt since 1975 [@zemp2026]. The source states the start of the cumulative period as 1975, while its figure of global mass change begins with the hydrological year 1976 [@zemp2026].

## 1.3 Glacier loss from 2000 to 2023

GlaMBIE estimates that glaciers lost on average 273 ± 16 Gt of mass per year from 2000 to 2023 [@glambie2025]. The cumulative loss over the period was 6,542 ± 387 Gt [@glambie2025]. This is about 5% of the global glacier mass in 2000 [@glambie2025].

The rate of loss increased over the period. Mass loss averaged 231 ± 23 Gt per year in 2000 to 2011 and 314 ± 23 Gt per year in 2012 to 2023, an increase of 36 ± 10% [@glambie2025]. The largest annual loss in the record was in 2023, at 548 ± 120 Gt [@glambie2025].

### 1.3.1 Mountain regions

Relative losses since 2000 range from 2% to 39% between regions [@glambie2025]. Central Europe lost 39% of its glacier ice, the largest relative loss of any region [@glambie2025]. Its glaciers lost 2.0 ± 0.1 Gt per year, or 1.06 ± 0.04 m water equivalent (w.e.) per year averaged over the glacier area [@glambie2025].

In High Mountain Asia, GlaMBIE reports separate results for three regions over 2000 to 2023 [@glambie2025]:

- Central Asia: a loss of 10.4 ± 2.7 Gt per year, or 0.22 ± 0.06 m w.e. per year [@glambie2025].
- South Asia West: a loss of 5.4 ± 1.6 Gt per year, or 0.17 ± 0.05 m w.e. per year [@glambie2025].
- South Asia East: a loss of 7.3 ± 1.2 Gt per year, or 0.52 ± 0.08 m w.e. per year [@glambie2025].

Mass loss in South Asia West increased in the second half of the record [@glambie2025]. Over 2000 to 2023, the largest regional shares of global glacier loss came from Alaska (22%), Arctic Canada (about 20%), the glaciers around Greenland (13%) and the Southern Andes (10%) [@glambie2025].

### 1.3.2 The IPCC assessment of high mountain glaciers

The IPCC Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere in a Changing Climate (SROCC) assessed glaciers in eleven high mountain regions. These cover all glacier regions except Antarctica, Greenland, the Canadian and Russian Arctic, and Svalbard [@hock2019srocc]. SROCC found that glaciers in high mountain areas have declined in recent decades due to climate change (*very high confidence*) [@hock2019srocc]. It found it *very likely* that atmospheric warming is the primary driver of the global recession of glaciers [@hock2019srocc].

For 2006 to 2015, SROCC assessed the mass budget of glaciers in these mountain regions as *very likely* −490 ± 100 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (−123 ± 24 Gt yr⁻¹) [@hock2019srocc]. Regional averages were *likely* most negative, below −850 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹, in the southern Andes, the Caucasus and the European Alps and Pyrenees [@hock2019srocc]. High Mountain Asia had the least negative average, at −150 ± 110 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹, with strong variation within the region [@hock2019srocc].

## 1.4 Recent years

In the hydrological year 2025, glaciers outside the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets lost 408 ± 132 Gt of mass [@zemp2026]. Six of the years with the highest mass loss on record fall within the past seven years [@zemp2026].

The pattern varied between regions in 2025. Loss per unit area was greatest in Western Canada and USA, Iceland and Central Europe [@zemp2026]. Compared with the 1991 to 2020 climate period, the largest departures from normal were in Western Canada and USA, South Asia West and Svalbard [@zemp2026]. The regions that added most to the global total were High Mountain Asia, Alaska and the Russian Arctic [@zemp2026].

The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) also reports on a set of reference glaciers with long-term measurements [@wmo2026sogc]. For these glaciers, the mass balance of the hydrological year 2024/2025 (September 2024 to August 2025) was among the five most negative in the record for 1950 to 2025 [@wmo2026sogc]. It was less negative than the record loss of 2022/2023 [@wmo2026sogc]. These data were not yet final when the report was prepared: 155 of the roughly 170 glaciers reported each year to the WGMS had submitted observations [@wmo2026sogc]. Eight of the ten most negative annual mass balances since 1950 have occurred since 2016 [@wmo2026sogc]. WMO identifies Iceland and the Pacific coast of North America as areas of exceptionally negative mass balance in 2025 [@wmo2026sogc].

## 1.5 Contribution to sea-level rise

The paper describing the WGMS method reports that glacier mass loss raised global mean sea level by 25.3 ± 1.7 mm between 1976 and 2024, or 0.5 ± 0.2 mm per year on average [@dussaillant2025essd]. About 10 mm of this rise came in the last ten years of that record, and about 1.5 mm in 2023 [@dussaillant2025essd].

GlaMBIE gives a contribution of 18 ± 1 mm from 2000 to 2023, an average of 0.75 ± 0.04 mm per year [@glambie2025]. The 2023 loss alone corresponds to 1.51 ± 0.33 mm [@glambie2025]. For 2002 to 2021, glaciers contributed 0.72 ± 0.04 mm per year, about 20% of the observed rise in sea level [@glambie2025]. Over the same period the Greenland Ice Sheet contributed 0.62 ± 0.06 mm per year, about 17% [@glambie2025]. The GlaMBIE authors find that glacier mass loss was about 18% larger than the loss from the Greenland Ice Sheet and more than twice the loss from the Antarctic Ice Sheet [@glambie2025].

WMO reports that ice loss from glaciers contributed around 21% of the total rise in sea level over 1993 to 2018 [@wmo2026sogc].

The loss in the hydrological year 2025 corresponds to 1.1 ± 0.4 mm of sea-level rise [@zemp2026]. The cumulative loss since 1975 corresponds to 26.4 ± 3.3 mm [@zemp2026].

For the eleven high mountain regions alone, SROCC gives a sea-level contribution of 0.34 ± 0.07 mm per year for 2006 to 2015 [@hock2019srocc].
